\begin{abstract}
%Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) in edge environments are vulnerable to \textbf{Targeted Bit-Flip Attacks (TBFAs)}. In multi-exit architectures, this vulnerability is amplified because adversaries can exploit shallow branches to force premature early exits.  While existing defenses introduce randomized dynamic early exits, they still rely on a fragile single-branch decision, allowing an attacker to compromise the entire system by attacking just one branch. To resolve this, we propose a resilient \textbf{Two-Phase Defense Architecture}. \textbf{Phase 1} introduces \textbf{Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE)}, which samples a subset of candidate branches and aggregates their predictions via a confidence-weighted consensus. \textbf{Phase 2} adds an on-device \textbf{Checksum-Based Self-Healing Mechanism} that verifies parameter integrity; upon detecting tampering, compromised branches are quarantined, allowing the remaining clean branches to self-heal predictions.  Evaluated on the CIFAR-10 benchmark, our architecture reduces the Attack Success Rate (ASR) to \textbf{15.09\%} while preserving a clean accuracy of \textbf{83.02\%} under aggressive attacks, demonstrating robust defense against physical bit-tampering.


Deep neural networks (DNNs) are increasingly used on edge devices, often sharing hardware resources with other applications and services. A bit-flip attack changes one or more bits in data involved in DNN inference. The attack does not require changes to the DNN software; flipping a few carefully selected bits in model weights, activations, or control data can change the final prediction. This makes DNNs running on exposed edge devices vulnerable to targeted bit-flip attacks (TBFAs), where specific bits are selected to cause misclassification or affect the reliability of inference. This vulnerability is particularly concerning in multi-exit architectures, where an attacker can target shallow branches and trigger incorrect early exits. Existing defenses based on randomized dynamic early exits reduce this risk to some extent, but the final decision still depends on a single selected branch. Consequently, compromising one branch can affect the overall prediction.

To address this limitation, we propose a two-phase defense architecture. In the first phase, Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE) selects a subset of candidate branches and combines their predictions using a confidence-weighted consensus. In the second phase, an on-device checksum-based self-healing mechanism monitors the integrity of model parameters. When tampering is detected, the affected branch is quarantined, and predictions are obtained from the remaining branches. We evaluate the proposed architecture on the CIFAR-10 dataset under aggressive bit-flip attacks. The proposed approach achieves an attack success rate (ASR) of 15.09\% while maintaining a clean accuracy of 83.02\%, indicating that the architecture can effectively limit the impact of physical bit tampering.


\end{abstract}

Recent studies have demonstrated that DNNs running on edge memory systems are particularly vulnerable to hardware-level fault injection attacks, notably Targeted Bit-Flip Attacks (TBFAs) exploiting DRAM disturbance vulnerabilities such as Rowhammer ~\cite{kim2014rowhammer}. By repeatedly accessing carefully selected DRAM rows, an adversary can induce bit flips in physically adjacent memory rows and thereby corrupt selected bits of DNN parameters stored in memory.

A bit-flip attack deliberately changes one or more bits in the data used during DNN inference. The attacker does not necessarily need to modify the DNN program itself; corrupting a few carefully selected bits in weights, activations, or control data can be enough to alter the final prediction. Despite the extremely small number of modified bits, the impact of such attacks can be substantial. In particular, an attacker may require fewer than twenty parameter-bit flips out of millions of parameters to implant a stealthy Trojan backdoor, as demonstrated by attacks such as the Targeted Bit-flip Trojan (TBT) ~\cite{rakin2020tbt} and ProFlip ~\cite{chen2021proflip}. These attacks strategically identify highly sensitive parameter bits whose alteration can significantly change the model's decision boundary while minimizing their impact on normal inference behavior. As a result, the compromised DNN can preserve high Clean Data Accuracy (CDA) on benign, untriggered inputs, making the attack difficult to detect using conventional accuracy-based validation. At the same time, inputs containing a carefully designed adversarial trigger can be systematically misclassified into an attacker-selected target class. The combination of stealthy model manipulation, hardware-level vulnerabilities, and targeted misclassification makes TBFA induced backdoors a serious security concern for DNNs deployed on resource constrained and safety critical edge systems.

To defend against TBFAs, the state-of-the-art framework \textbf{Aegis} \cite{wang2023aegis} uses a randomized dynamic early-exit mechanism. At each inference, it randomly selects $C$ candidate branches and terminates at the first branch whose confidence exceeds a threshold of $\tau = 0.95$. However, this design has a key weakness, which we refer to as \textbf{single-branch decision dependency}. Since the final prediction is determined by the first candidate branch that meets the threshold, compromising a single shallow branch can be sufficient to influence the prediction of the entire network. Under white-box adaptive attacks where the attacker optimizes bit flips against all internal branches, the Attack Success Rate (ASR) defined as the fraction of triggered inputs misclassified into the attacker's target class of Aegis escalates dramatically to 31.10\% on CIFAR-10, with significant clean accuracy degradation.

To eliminate these fundamental bottlenecks, we propose \textbf{ResilientDNN}, a resilient two-phase defense architecture that combines stochastic ensembling with autonomous on-device self-healing.

Main contributions of our work are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 - Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE):} We eliminate the fragile single-branch decision rule by introducing dynamic multi-branch soft consensus. For each incoming input $x$, SBE randomly samples $C = 5$ candidate branches from a pre-screened pool $\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}$ and aggregates their predictions using confidence-weighted averaging. This eliminates the brittle static threshold $\tau = 0.95$ and dilutes the influence of isolated compromised branches.
    
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 - Checksum-Based Self-Healing Quarantine:} To defeat adaptive adversaries who jointly optimize bit flips across branches, Phase 2 integrates an on-device parameter integrity verification system. Leveraging the deterministic byte representation of 8-bit quantized weights ($W_q \in \mathbb{Z}^8$), cryptographic SHA-256 digests are verified against immutable golden fingerprints in secure memory. Once bit tampering is detected, the corrupted branch is quarantined via a binary trust mask ($\mu_k = 0$), enabling the network to self-heal predictions with zero retraining and zero rollback overhead.
    
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Dual-Algorithm Formalization:} We formalize the defense into two autonomous, modular algorithms: Algorithm~\ref{alg:sbe_inference} (governing Phase 1 stochastic ensemble inference) and Algorithm~\ref{alg:checksum_quarantine} (executing Phase 2 intra-model checksum verification and safe pool quarantine).
    
    \item \textbf{Negligible Edge Hardware Overhead:} We show that storing golden hashes for all 16 IC heads requires merely $512\text{ bytes}$ of secure ROM/TPM storage, and on-device hash verification consumes less than $5\,\mu\text{s}$ per IC, guaranteeing minimal computational and memory burden for edge deployment.
    
    \item \textbf{Extensive Empirical Validation:} We benchmark our architecture on quantized ResNet-32 on CIFAR-10 under non-adaptive and white-box adaptive TBT attacks. ResilientDNN slashes adaptive ASR from 38.50\% (Aegis baseline) to \textbf{15.09\%} while preserving clean accuracy at \textbf{83.02\%}, outperforming all prior binarization and dynamic early-exit baselines.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adversary Objectives}
Let $f(\cdot; \mathcal{W}): \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ denote a quantized multi-exit DNN parameterized by weights $\mathcal{W}$. The adversary seeks to perturb a minimal subset of parameter bits $\Delta \mathcal{W}$ in physical memory such that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Targeted Misclassification:} For any input $x \in \mathcal{X}$ patched with a backdoor trigger pattern $\delta$ ($x' = x \oplus \delta$), the model outputs the attacker-chosen target label $y_t$:
    \begin{equation}
        f(x \oplus \delta; \mathcal{W} \oplus \Delta \mathcal{W}) = y_t, \quad \text{where } y_t \neq y_{\text{true}}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Clean Data Stealthiness:} On clean, unpoisoned samples ($x \in \mathcal{X}$), the model preserves its original Clean Data Accuracy (CDA):
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{P}\left(f(x; \mathcal{W} \oplus \Delta \mathcal{W}) = y_{\text{true}}\right) \approx \mathbb{P}\left(f(x; \mathcal{W}) = y_{\text{true}}\right).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Adversary Capabilities and Constraints}
We adopt the standard physical memory fault injection threat model widely assumed in hardware security literature~\cite{rakin2020tbt, wang2023aegis}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hardware Bit-Flip Budget:} The adversary cannot arbitrarily overwrite full floating-point matrices. Instead, bit-tampering is constrained to a tiny budget $N_b \le 20$ bits out of millions of model parameter bits, executed via DRAM disturbance techniques (e.g., Rowhammer) without requiring root OS access.
    \item \textbf{Quantized Memory Access:} The target DNN is deployed in 8-bit integer quantized format ($W_q \in \mathbb{Z}^8$). The adversary leverages Neural Gradient Ranking (NGR) or progressive bit search to identify and flip the most sensitive sign and high-order bits.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Attack Knowledge Settings}
We evaluate two rigorous adversarial knowledge regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-Adaptive Attacks:} The adversary generates the trigger and identifies critical bits assuming a standard undefended model, targeting parameters in dense or final layers without specific awareness of dynamic branching mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Attacks:} The adversary possesses full white-box knowledge of the network architecture, candidate IC pools, and dynamic branch selection strategies. The attacker explicitly optimizes the loss function jointly across all candidate internal classifiers to ensure that poisoned feature representations bypass dynamic sampling.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Existing Solutions}

Hardware-level attacks expose a fundamental vulnerability of DNNs by exploiting faults in memories, weights, and computation rather than modifying the model software.
Prior studies have shown that techniques such as RowHammer and fault injection can induce targeted bit flips in DNN parameters and significantly alter inference outcomes \cite{hong2019terminal,yao2020deephammer}.
These attacks are particularly concerning because carefully selected flips can cause severe degradation or targeted misclassification with only a small number of corrupted bits.
Recent work has therefore explored hardware-assisted defenses to detect, tolerate, or recover from such faults, highlighting the need for resilient DNN architectures \cite{luo2021deepstrike,khoshavi2022hardenn}.

\subsection{Multi-Exit Neural Networks}
Multi-exit architectures, such as Shallow-Deep Networks (SDNs)~\cite{kaya2019sdn} and BranchyNet~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet}, augment a feed-forward backbone by attaching $B$ internal classifier (IC) heads $\{B_0, B_1, \dots, B_{B-1}\}$ to intermediate hidden layers, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:sdn_architecture}. 

\begin{comment}


Each IC branch maps intermediate representations into class prediction probabilities, enabling ``easy'' samples to exit early, reducing computational latency and memory bandwidth consumption on edge hardware. Key properties of SDNs~\cite{kaya2019sdn} include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Inference Depth:} SDNs allow each input to exit at an intermediate layer when the attached IC produces a sufficiently confident prediction, avoiding unnecessary computation through deeper layers.
    \item \textbf{Overthinking Mitigation:} By enabling early exits, SDNs reduce the ``negative overthinking'' phenomenon where additional depth degrades prediction quality on already well-classified samples.
    \item \textbf{Lightweight IC Design:} Each internal classifier typically consists of a small convolutional feature reduction block followed by a fully connected projection layer, adding minimal parameter overhead to the backbone.
    \item \textbf{Joint Training Objective:} All ICs are trained jointly with the final classifier using a weighted cross-entropy loss, ensuring that intermediate representations remain discriminative at every exit point.
    \item \textbf{Latency-Accuracy Trade-off:} SDNs provide a configurable trade-off between inference latency and prediction accuracy by adjusting the confidence threshold $\tau$ that governs early-exit decisions.
\end{itemize}
\end{comment}

Each IC takes the intermediate feature representation produced by the backbone and generates class probabilities. If the prediction is sufficiently confident, the input can exit at that point instead of being processed by the remaining layers, thereby reducing inference latency and memory traffic on edge devices. The main characteristics of SDNs \cite{kaya2019sdn} are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Adaptive Inference Depth:} An input can exit at an intermediate layer when the corresponding IC produces a sufficiently confident prediction. This avoids unnecessary computation in the deeper layers.

\item \textbf{Reduced Overthinking:} Early exits can avoid the ``negative overthinking'' effect, in which processing an already correctly classified sample through additional layers may reduce the prediction quality.

\item \textbf{Lightweight ICs:} An IC generally uses a small convolutional feature-reduction block followed by a fully connected layer. Thus, the additional parameters introduced by the intermediate classifiers are relatively small compared with the backbone network.

\item \textbf{Joint Training:} The ICs and the final classifier are trained together using a weighted cross-entropy loss. This allows the intermediate layers to learn representations that are useful for classification at different depths.

\item \textbf{Latency-Accuracy Trade-off:} The confidence threshold $\tau$ controls when an input exits the network. Changing this threshold provides a trade-off between inference latency and prediction accuracy.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Current Defense Baseline: Aegis}
To mitigate targeted bit-flip attacks, the state-of-the-art framework \textbf{Aegis}~\cite{wang2023aegis} introduces a \textit{Randomized Dynamic Early-Exit} mechanism over an SDN backbone; The Aegis uses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Branch Logit Computation:} The network produces intermediate logit vectors $\mathbf{z}(x) = [z_0(x), \dots, z_{B-1}(x)]$. Branch softmax probabilities and peak confidences are obtained as:
    \begin{equation}
        P_i(x) = \text{Softmax}(z_i(x)), \quad c_i(x) = \max_{c} P_{i, c}(x).
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Randomized Branch Sampling:} Aegis uniformly samples a candidate subset of $C = 3$ branches from a pre-selected high-performing pool $\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}} \subset \{B_0, \dots, B_{B-1}\}$ to introduce runtime execution graph unpredictability.
    \item \textbf{Ordered Sequential Evaluation:} The candidate branches are sorted strictly by physical network depth from shallowest to deepest: $S = (s_1, s_2, \dots, s_C)$, where $\text{depth}(s_1) < \dots < \text{depth}(s_C)$.
    \item \textbf{Early-Exit Decision Rule:} Candidate branches in $S$ are evaluated one-by-one. Inference terminates at the first branch whose confidence satisfies:
    \begin{equation}
        i^* = \min \left\{ i \in \{1, \dots, C\} \mid c_{s_i}(x) \ge \tau \right\},
    \end{equation}
    yielding output $\hat{y} = \hat{y}_{s_{i^*}}$. If no candidate branch crosses $\tau = 0.95$, inference defaults to the deepest candidate branch: $\hat{y} = \hat{y}_{s_C}$.
\end{enumerate}

Furthermore, the defense is fragilely bound to the rigid static threshold $\tau = 0.95$, which cannot adapt to input complexity or domain shifts.

Since inference stops at the first branch whose confidence exceeds $\tau$, compromising only one shallow candidate branch can affect the final prediction. \section{Proposed Two-Phase Defense Architecture}

The remaining trusted branches are then passed to Phase 1, the Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE) module. \subsection{Phase 1: Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE)}
%Instead of terminating early upon encountering an isolated overconfident branch, SBE evaluates all sampled candidate branches concurrently and aggregates their predictions through confidence-weighted soft consensus. The complete procedure is formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sbe_inference}.

Rather than terminating inference when a single branch produces a high confidence prediction, SBE evaluates all the sampled candidate branches and combines their outputs using a simple confidence weighted soft consensus. The resulting inference procedure is described in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sbe_inference}.


\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Candidate Pool Pruning:} On multi-exit backbones (e.g., 16-branch ResNet-32), early ICs attached to initial layers lack sufficient depth for robust feature representation ($\sim$10\% clean accuracy). We evaluate clean validation accuracy across all branches and prune the bottom $M$ weakest classifiers ($M = 4$), establishing a high-performing candidate pool. Here, $\gamma$ denotes the minimum clean validation accuracy threshold required for a branch to be admitted into the candidate pool; branches scoring below $\gamma$ are deemed too weak for reliable inference and are pruned:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}_{\text{active}} = \{B_i \mid \text{Acc}(B_i) \ge \gamma\}, \quad |\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}| = B - M = 12.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Subset Selection:} For each input $x$, we draw an expanded subset $S$ of $C = 5$ candidate branches uniformly at random without replacement:
    \begin{equation}
        S = \{B_{(1)}, B_{(2)}, \dots, B_{(C)}\} \sim \text{Uniform}(\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}, C).
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Confidence-Weighted Soft Consensus:} For each sampled branch $k \in S$, we obtain class probabilities $P_k(x) = \text{Softmax}(z_k(x))$ and peak confidence weight $w_k = \max_c P_{k, c}(x)$. Predictions are aggregated via confidence-weighted averaging:
    \begin{equation}
        P_{\text{ens}}(x) = \frac{\sum_{k \in S} w_k \cdot P_k(x)}{\sum_{k \in S} w_k}, \quad \hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} P_{\text{ens}, c}(x).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
By requiring a multi-branch consensus, SBE ensures that an isolated hijacked branch cannot dictate the classification outcome, as its vote is diluted and outvoted by the remaining clean branches in $S$. Furthermore, SBE completely eliminates the brittle static threshold $\tau$. 

For example, if 5 branches predict Tiger, Tiger, Cat, Tiger, and Cat, the SBE selects Tiger by majority vote even if one branch is hijacked. Unlike a fixed threshold $\tau$, SBE relies on branch consensus, so the decision remains robust as long as the clean branches agree.


\begin{algorithm}[tb!]
\caption{Phase 1: Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE) Inference}
\label{alg:sbe_inference}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
\REQUIRE Input sample $x$, active candidate pool $\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}$, ensemble size $C$
\ENSURE Consensus class prediction $\hat{y}$
\STATE \textit{// Step 1: Dynamic Random Branch Sub-sampling}
\STATE $C' \leftarrow \min(C, |\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}|)$
\STATE $S \leftarrow \text{UniformRandomSample}(\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}, \text{size}=C')$
\STATE \textit{// Step 2: Concurrent Multi-Branch Forward Evaluation}
\STATE $Logits \leftarrow [\:\:]$
\FOR{each branch $B_k \in S$}
    \STATE $z_k \leftarrow \text{ForwardBranch}(x, B_k)$
    \STATE $Logits\text{.append}(z_k)$
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textit{// Step 3: Probability \& Peak Confidence Estimation}
\STATE $Probs \leftarrow [\:\:]$, $Weights \leftarrow [\:\:]$
\FOR{each $z_k \in Logits$}
    \STATE $P_k \leftarrow \text{Softmax}(z_k)$
    \STATE $w_k \leftarrow \max_c P_{k, c}$ \quad \textit{// Peak top-1 confidence weight}
    \STATE $Probs\text{.append}(P_k)$, $Weights\text{.append}(w_k)$
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textit{// Step 4: Confidence-Weighted Soft Consensus}
\STATE $P_{\text{ens}}(x) \leftarrow \frac{\sum_{k \in S} w_k \cdot P_k}{\sum_{k \in S} w_k}$
\STATE $\hat{y} \leftarrow \arg\max_c P_{\text{ens}, c}(x)$
\RETURN $\hat{y}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Phase 2: Checksum-Based Self-Healing Quarantine}
While SBE effectively neutralizes non-adaptive attacks, an adaptive adversary with white-box knowledge of the ensemble can optimize joint bit flips across multiple branches simultaneously. To counteract this, Phase 2 incorporates an on-device \textbf{Weight Integrity Verification and Self-Healing Quarantine} mechanism, formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:checksum_quarantine}.

\begin{algorithm}[tb!]
\caption{Phase 2: Intra-Model IC Checksum Verification and Quarantine}
\label{alg:checksum_quarantine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
\REQUIRE Multi-exit model $M$ with IC branches $\{B_0, \dots, B_{B-1}\}$, candidate pool $\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}$, golden fingerprints $\{h_k^*\}_{k=0}^{B-1}$
\ENSURE Sanitized candidate pool $\mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}}$, trust mask $\boldsymbol{\mu}$
\STATE \textbf{Initialize:} $\mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}} \leftarrow \emptyset$, $\boldsymbol{\mu} \leftarrow \mathbf{0}^B$
\STATE \textit{// Step 1: Periodic On-Device IC Parameter Verification}
\FOR{each IC branch $B_k \in \{B_0, \dots, B_{B-1}\}$}
    \STATE $b_k \leftarrow \text{bytes}(W_{q, k})$ \quad \textit{// Extract quantized weight bytes}
    \STATE $h_k \leftarrow \text{SHA-256}(b_k)$
    \IF{$h_k == h_k^*$}
        \STATE $\mu_k \leftarrow 1$; \textit{//Branch verified intact}
    \ELSE
        \STATE $\mu_k \leftarrow 0$; \textit{//Bit tampering detected quarantine branch}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textit{// Step 2: Construct Sanitized Safe Candidate Pool}
\FOR{each $B_k \in \mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}$}
    \IF{$\mu_k == 1$}
        \STATE $\mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}} \cup \{B_k\}$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textit{// Step 3: Extreme Fallback Safety Check}
\IF{$\mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}} == \emptyset$}
    \STATE $\mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}} \leftarrow \{B_{\text{final}}\}$ \quad \textit{// Fallback to deepest backbone exit}
\ENDIF
\RETURN $\mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}}, \boldsymbol{\mu}$ \quad \textit{// Passed to Algorithm~\ref{alg:sbe_inference} as $\mathcal{B}_{\text{active}}$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quantized Deterministic Parameter Hashing:} In quantized neural networks, weight parameters are mapped to fixed-point integer representations ($W_q \in \mathbb{Z}^b$, e.g., 8-bit signed integers). This integer representation eliminates floating-point non-determinism across varying hardware accelerators. At deployment, a cryptographic golden hash fingerprint $h_k^*$ is computed over the serialized parameter bytes of each IC module $k \in \{0, \dots, B-1\}$ using SHA-256:
    \begin{equation}
        h_k^* = \text{SHA-256}\left(\text{bytes}(W_{q, k})\right),
    \end{equation}
    and stored securely in tamper-proof read-only hardware memory (e.g., TPM, Secure Enclave, or ROM) \cite{rangarajan2022supervault}.
    \item \textbf{Runtime Tamper Detection \& Trust Masking:} During inference, the system periodically recomputes current hash digests $h_k = \text{SHA-256}(\text{bytes}(W_{q, k}))$ across all internal classifier heads. A binary trust mask $\mu_k \in \{0, 1\}$ is assigned to each branch:
    \begin{equation}
        \mu_k = \mathbb{I}\left( h_k == h_k^* \right) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if branch } k \text{ is intact} \\ 0, & \text{if bit-tampering detected.} \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Trust-Masked Quarantine and Self-Healing Consensus:} Any branch detected with bit-level corruption ($\mu_k = 0$) is immediately quarantined and removed from the active candidate sampling pool:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}} = \{ B_k \in \mathcal{B}_{\text{active}} \mid \mu_k = 1 \}.
    \end{equation}
    The active ensemble subset is dynamically sampled strictly from healthy branches ($S_{\text{safe}} \subseteq \mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}}$, with $|S_{\text{safe}}| = \min(C, |\mathcal{B}_{\text{safe}}|)$), producing the self-healed consensus:
    \begin{equation}
        P_{\text{healed}}(x) = \frac{\sum_{k \in S_{\text{safe}}} (\mu_k \cdot w_k) \cdot P_k(x)}{\sum_{k \in S_{\text{safe}}} (\mu_k \cdot w_k)}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Because clean ICs seamlessly absorb the inference load, the network self-heals predictions with \textbf{zero retraining, zero parameter rollback overhead}, and negligible cryptographic overhead ($<5\,\mu\text{s}$ check per IC).

\section{Experimental Results and Comparison}

\subsection{Experimental Settings}
We evaluated our defense against Targeted Bit-flip Trojan (TBT) attacks using a quantized ResNet-32 backbone on the \textbf{CIFAR-10} ($32 \times 32$, 10 classes) benchmark. All experiments were executed on an enterprise GPU cluster using NVIDIA Tesla P100-16GB and H100 GPUs under PyTorch 1.13.1 and CUDA 11.7. 
The ResNet-32 model was trained for 200 epochs using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with momentum 0.9, weight decay $3 \times 10^{-4}$, initial learning rate 0.01 with schedule decay at epochs 100 and 150, achieving a peak clean baseline validation accuracy of 91.38\%. The TBT attack was configured with target class $y_t = 2$ and an $11 \times 11$ trigger patch. The Trojan Area Percentage (TAP) defined as the ratio of the trigger patch area to the total input image area was $\text{TAP} \approx 11.8\%$.

Despite a 91.38\% clean validation accuracy, the model is exposed to a relatively small 11×11 trigger occupying only 11.8\% of the image, highlighting how little input manipulation may be needed to influence DNN behavior. The targeted class setting shows that the attack is not merely degrading accuracy; it is designed to steer specific inputs toward a chosen miss classification target.

\subsection{Comparison Against Binarization (BIN, RA-BNN)} 

Weight-binarization methods perform poorly against targeted attacks, with ASRs of $94.80\%$ and $74.50\%$, respectively. This vulnerability can be attributed to the increased sensitivity of critical-path parameters when the weights are constrained to $\pm 1$.  

Weight binarization alone does not provide effective protection against targeted attacks, as BIN and RA-BNN still achieve high attack success rates of 94.80\% and 74.50\%, respectively. Restricting weights to $\pm1$ can make a small set of critical parameters more influential, allowing targeted bit flips to cause substantial changes in the model's prediction. Which make it very bad choice for edge deployment when security is a major concern.

\subsection{Non-Adaptive Attack Resilience} 

The unaugmented BASE model suffers $70.70\%$ ASR. Dynamic multi-exit SDN and Aegis lower ASR to $16.30\%$ and $19.90\%$ (with $17.82\%$ in reproduced single-model runs). Phase 1 (SBE) reduces ASR to $15.48\%$ while boosting clean accuracy to $90.01\%$. The two-phase defense substantially improves resilience, reducing ASR from 70.70\% to 11.67\%, while preserving clean accuracy high. Self-healing provides an additional layer of protection beyond multi-exit consensus, reducing the residual attack success by a further 24.6\% relative to Phase 1.
 
 \subsection{ Adaptive Attack Neutralization} 
    
Under adaptive attacks where the adversary optimizes bit flips jointly against all internal branches, Aegis suffers $31.10\%$ ASR (and $38.50\%$ in reproduced single-model evaluation with clean accuracy falling to $83.41\%$), and undefended SBE reaches $40.72\%$. Adaptive attacks are particularly challenging because the adversary knows the model structure and its mitigation mechanisms, enabling bit flips to be optimized jointly against all internal branches. Even under this stronger threat model, Phase 2 self-healing reduces ASR from 40.72\% to 15.09\% (60.8\% reduction) while maintaining 83.02\% clean accuracy, bringing the attack success rate close to the 10\% random-guess bound. Blue line is consistently above 90\%.

\section{Overhead and Security Discussion}

\subsection{Hardware Storage and Latency Overhead}
Our Phase 2 integrity verification imposes negligible hardware overhead. Because SHA-256 fingerprints are computed exclusively over the compact parameter tensors of the 16 IC heads (linear projection layers containing $64 \times 10 = 640$ weights each), storing all 16 golden hashes requires only $16 \times 32\text{ bytes} = \mathbf{512\text{ bytes}}$ in secure read-only memory (ROM/TPM). Computing SHA-256 over 640 bytes takes less than $5\,\mu\text{s}$ on standard edge processors, incurring less than $0.08\,\text{ms}$ total verification overhead for all ICs combined.

\subsection{Deterministic Quantization Guarantee}
A fundamental challenge in cryptographic verification of neural networks is floating-point non-determinism across heterogeneous edge hardware. In our architecture, models are deployed in fixed-point 8-bit integer quantization ($W_q \in \mathbb{Z}^8$). Because integer byte representations are strictly deterministic and invariant to micro-architectural differences, the SHA-256 digest produces zero false-positive integrity alarms across arbitrary deployment platforms.

\subsection{Attack-Agnostic Defense Capability}
Because our Phase 2 verification operates directly on physical parameter bitstrings in memory, the integrity check is inherently \textbf{attack-algorithm-agnostic}. Whether an adversary attempts targeted backdoor injection via TBT~\cite{rakin2020tbt}, progressive parameter search via ProFlip~\cite{chen2021proflip}, latent feature manipulation via TA-LBF~\cite{bai2021talbf}, or untargeted BFA~\cite{rakin2019bfa}, any unauthorized modification to physical weight bits immediately invalidates the cryptographic digest. The tampered branch get quarantined before the malicious activations can propagate into the stochastic ensemble.

\section{Conclusion}

%We  presented a resilient Two-Phase Defense Architecture against physical parameter tampering and Targeted Bit-flip Trojan (TBT) attacks in multi-exit neural networks. By combining Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE) with an on-device cryptographic checksum verification and dynamic quarantine mechanism, our approach effectively eliminates the single-branch failure bottleneck inherent in existing early-exit defenses like Aegis. When an adaptive adversary attempts to hijack intermediate representations, our defense dynamically detects bit tampering, quarantines compromised branches, and routes inference through safe IC consensus with zero retraining. Evaluations on CIFAR-10 with quantized ResNet-32 demonstrate that our defense neutralizes adaptive attacks down to 15.09\% ASR while preserving high clean data accuracy at 83.02\%, establishing a robust foundation for secure edge intelligence.

We presented a two-phase defense architecture for protecting multi-exit neural networks against physical parameter tampering and targeted bit-flip Trojan (TBT) attacks. The approach combines Stochastic Branch Ensembling (SBE) with on-device cryptographic checksum verification and branch quarantine to address the single-branch dependency of existing early-exit defenses such as Aegis. During inference, the checksum mechanism identifies tampered branches and removes them from further use, while SBE combines predictions from the remaining trusted ICs without requiring model retraining. Experiments on quantized ResNet-32 using CIFAR-10 show that the proposed defense reduces the ASR of adaptive attacks to 15.09\% while maintaining a clean accuracy of 83.02\%. 

 In future,  we will evaluate the proposed defense on larger DNN models and a wider range of datasets, including networks with different numbers and placements of intermediate classifiers. We plan to explore lightweight and adaptive checksum mechanisms to reduce verification overhead and make the approach more suitable for resource-constrained edge devices.
